Turning 65? Here's how to start Medicare without missing a step.

By Christopher Apana, Florida license W197973, NPN 17317513.

You get one seven-month window to sign up for Medicare on your own schedule. Miss it and you can pay a penalty for life. This guide walks through the window, the 2027 costs, your three ways to fill Medicare's gaps, and a checklist you can print. If you'd rather talk it through, call (561) 918-2999 and a licensed agent in Boca Raton will.

Your seven-month window

Your Initial Enrollment Period starts three months before the month you turn 65 and ends three months after it. Sign up in the three months before, and coverage starts the first day of your birthday month. Sign up during or after, and coverage starts the first of the next month.

Born on the 1st of the month? Medicare counts you as turning 65 the month before, so your window starts and ends one month earlier than shown.

What Medicare costs in 2027

Part AHospital

Most people pay no premium. Hospital deductible $1,776 for each benefit period. In a skilled nursing facility, days 21 to 100 cost $222 a day.

Part BDoctors and outpatient

$209.40 a month, with a $292 yearly deductible. Higher incomes pay more.

Part CMedicare Advantage

Not an extra bill: another way to get Part A and Part B. You keep paying the Part B premium. Each plan sets its own premium and copays, and no plan can charge more than $9,850 a year for covered in-network care.

Part DPrescriptions

Premium varies by plan. Deductible up to $700. Out-of-pocket drug costs capped at $2,400.

Part A and Part B amounts are from CMS, released November 17, 2026. How Medicare Advantage works.

Three ways to fill the gaps

  • Original Medicare with a Medigap plan and a Part D drug planAny doctor who takes Medicare, predictable costs, a separate monthly premium. See the Medicare Supplement guide.
  • A Medicare Advantage planOne card, often drug coverage included, a network of doctors and a yearly out-of-pocket limit. See the Medicare Advantage guide.
  • Keep employer coverage for nowIf you or your spouse still work at a company with 20 or more employees, you may be able to delay Part B without a penalty. See Working past 65.

10%

Part B late penalty

Of the national base premium for each month you went without drug coverage that's at least as good as Medicare's.

Medicare for veterans: how it fits with the benefits you earned.

By Christopher Apana, Florida license W197973, NPN 17317513.

VA health care and Medicare are separate systems, and neither pays the other's bills. Many veterans in Broward and Palm Beach keep both, so they can use VA facilities and also see doctors outside the VA. This guide covers how each benefit treats Medicare and what to decide at 65.

Three benefits, three sets of rules

VA health care

You aren't required to take Medicare. But Medicare doesn't pay for care at VA facilities, and VA benefits generally don't pay for care outside the VA unless the VA authorizes it.

TRICARE For Life

You need both Medicare Part A and Part B to have it. Medicare pays first and TRICARE For Life pays second.

CHAMPVA

Once you're eligible for Medicare at 65, you need Part A and Part B to keep CHAMPVA. It then pays after Medicare.

The Part B decision

VA health care on its own doesn't protect you from the Part B late penalty. If you skip Part B at 65 and want it later, your premium goes up 10% for each full 12 months you waited, and you may have to wait for the General Enrollment Period to sign up.

Having Part B means you can see any doctor or hospital that takes Medicare, which matters if the nearest VA facility is a long drive or if VA funding or your priority group changes.

Prescriptions and Part D

VA drug coverage counts as creditable coverage. If you have it, you can go without a Part D plan and not owe the Part D late penalty if you join one later. Some veterans still add drug coverage so they can fill prescriptions from non-VA doctors at a local pharmacy.

Long-term care, life and critical illness insurance.

By Christopher Apana, Florida license W197973, NPN 17317513.

These are separate insurance products. They are not Medicare, they don't fill Medicare's gaps, and nobody needs them to get Medicare help from us.

This page is not about Medicare. To see what Medicare pays for long-term care and every way to cover the rest, including Medicaid and VA benefits, read What Medicare doesn't cover.

Long-term care insurance

Long-term care insurance helps pay for help with everyday activities, such as bathing, dressing and eating, at home, in assisted living or in a nursing home. Benefits usually start when you need help with two of six daily activities or have a severe cognitive impairment, and after a waiting period you choose.

  • Premiums are lower when you buy younger and healthier, and you have to qualify medically.
  • Some life insurance policies include long-term care benefits instead of a separate policy.
  • Florida's Long-Term Care Partnership program lets some policies protect part of your savings if you later need Medicaid.

Life insurance (final expense)

Final expense is a smaller whole life policy meant for funeral costs and final bills. The benefit is paid to the people you name, and they can use it however they need.

  • The premium usually stays the same, and coverage doesn't expire while premiums are paid.
  • Many policies ask a few health questions instead of a medical exam. Guaranteed issue policies ask none, but usually pay a limited benefit in the first two to three years.

Critical illness: cancer, heart attack and stroke

These policies pay a cash amount if you're diagnosed with a covered condition. They don't replace health coverage, and they pay on top of it. You decide how to spend the money, for example on travel for treatment or bills while you recover.

How we talk about these

  • We never discuss them during a Medicare appointment. If you raise one there, we set up a separate appointment for another day.
  • You don't have to buy anything, and you can stop the conversation at any time.

Retirement income: covering the bills that can't wait.

By Christopher Apana, Florida license W197973, NPN 17317513.

This page is about retirement income and annuities. It is not about Medicare. Nothing here is part of a Medicare plan review, and we never bring it up in a Medicare appointment.

Most retirement plans start with one question: will the money that comes in every month, for as long as you live, cover the bills you can't skip?

Start with the gap

Step 1Your essential bills

Housing, food, utilities, insurance, medicine. The things you pay every month no matter what.

Step 2Your guaranteed income

Social Security, a pension, or anything else paid for life.

Step 3The difference

If income covers the bills, you may not need anything else. If it doesn't, that gap is what to plan for.

Ways to close it

An annuity is one option, and often not the first. Others include waiting to claim Social Security, choosing a pension payout that continues for a spouse, cutting a fixed cost, or drawing a set amount from savings. We'll talk through all of them.

The kinds of annuities we discuss

An annuity is a contract with an insurance company. You pay in, all at once or over time, and it can pay you income later, for a set number of years or for life.

We offer fixed and fixed indexed annuities only. We do not sell variable annuities or registered index-linked annuities.

When an annuity is the wrong answer

  • You may need the money for emergencies or large costs in the next several years.
  • Your guaranteed income already covers your essential bills.
  • You'd be paying surrender charges to leave another annuity.
  • You don't have enough savings outside the annuity to feel secure.
  • You're being rushed, or you don't understand the contract yet.

Our duty to you

  • Florida law requires us to act in your best interest when we recommend an annuity. We review your income, savings, needs and goals first, and we'll tell you if an annuity doesn't fit.
